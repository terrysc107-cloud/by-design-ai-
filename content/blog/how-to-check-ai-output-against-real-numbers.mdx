The failure mode people expect from AI is gibberish. That is not what you get.

What you get is a confident, well-written paragraph that could be about any business and
quietly is not about yours. It reads well. It sounds like analysis. And somewhere in the
middle of it is a number that nothing produced.

Articulate is free. Correct is the thing you have to check for.

## Why a plausible number looks exactly like a real one

If you ask any assistant to analyse a business and there is a hole in the data, it will
produce something reasonable-sounding to fill the hole. It is not lying. It is completing
a pattern, which is what it does, and a completed pattern reads identically to a
retrieved fact.

On the page, "revenue was around $8,400 this month" looks the same whether it came from
your books or from the shape of the sentence. There is no visual difference. That is why
you need a check rather than a feeling.

## The four checks

Run these on your most recent output. It takes under a minute.

**1. Does every claim trace to a number in your files?**
Pick one specific figure in the report and try to find where it came from. If you cannot
locate it in something you maintain, it was generated.

**2. Did it name what it did not have?**
This is the one that usually fails. A good report says "I do not have last month's
close rate, so I have not calculated trend." A bad one quietly calculates trend anyway.
Absence of any such statement is a warning sign, not a clean bill of health, because
there is always something missing.

**3. Is it consistent with decisions you already made?**
If you killed a channel in March, a report recommending you double down on it in
September has not read your history. It is analysing a snapshot, not your business.

**4. Could you act on it today?**
Not "is it interesting." Could you do something. A report that produces no possible
action is a summary of things you already knew, phrased well.

## Three of the four are fixed by one sentence

Add this to whatever instructions your assistant reads:

> If a number you need is missing, say it is missing. Do not estimate it.

That single line fixes check two directly, and it fixes checks one and four as a side
effect, because a report that flags its own gaps stops producing confident conclusions
built on invented inputs.

It is a small instruction that does an unreasonable amount of work, and almost nobody
writes it, because it does not occur to you to forbid something you did not know was
happening.

## Record the query next to the number

The deeper version of check one: whenever a number comes from somewhere real, write down
*how it was retrieved*, right beside the value.

It looks redundant when you do it. It is the difference between a number a future reader
can verify and a number they have to trust. Six weeks later, you are that future reader,
and you will not remember.
