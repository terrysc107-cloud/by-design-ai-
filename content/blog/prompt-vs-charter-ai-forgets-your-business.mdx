Here is a test. Open a new chat with whatever AI you use and ask it something about your
business.

If your first message has to explain what you do, who your customers are, and what you
are trying to work out, you are writing prompts. And prompts do not accumulate. Every
session starts from zero, produces something generic, and teaches the system nothing it
keeps.

## The difference

A **prompt** is what you ask this time. It lives in one conversation and dies with it.

A **charter** is who the seat is. It is a file the assistant reads before every single
run, so the context is already there and the request can be short.

That is the entire shift, and it is less work than it sounds. You are already explaining
your business every time. A charter is that explanation, written once, in a file.

## The five sections

**Identity.** What this seat owns. Not "marketing" but the specific thing: revenue truth,
or pipeline hygiene, or what came in and what is about to lapse.

**Disposition.** How it decides when two reasonable options conflict. More on this below,
because it is the one that matters.

**Mandate.** Three to five things it is for. Fewer than you want. A seat that is for
everything is for nothing.

**Guardrails.** What it must never claim and never do. This is where you write down that
it does not invent numbers and does not send anything.

**Cadence.** When it runs.

## Disposition is the line that does the work

Most people write disposition as a list of adjectives. Thoughtful. Strategic.
Detail-oriented. Those describe nobody and change nothing, because no reasonable system
was going to be thoughtless.

A disposition line is doing its job when it names something the seat will **refuse**,
including something it would otherwise prefer.

Here is a real one:

> A retention-first performance marketer. Kills formats and campaigns that do not
> perform, including her own favourites.

That last clause is the whole thing. It tells the seat that consistency with its own past
recommendations is worth less than evidence. That is a genuinely hard instruction, and it
changes what comes back: the seat will now argue against something it suggested last
month, which is exactly the behaviour you cannot get from a prompt.

## Test yours in one question

Read your disposition line and ask: does it name something this seat will refuse, even
when it would rather not?

If the answer is no, you have written a description, not an instruction. Write it again.

The quickest way in is to think of a decision you have watched go wrong, and write the
line that would have stopped it.
